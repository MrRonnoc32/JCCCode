A working analysis

What is the impact on Jacksonville if property taxes are cut?

Before you vote on the property-tax amendment, see what Jacksonville would have to cut or replace. Florida's proposed homestead exemption would phase up to the first $250,000 of a home's assessed value, reducing Jacksonville's budget by about $283 million a year. This site lays out the tradeoff so you can weigh it for yourself.

1 · The core problem

Covering the cut means cutting deep into city services

The proposed exemption would reduce Jacksonville's General Fund revenue by about $283 million a year. Public safety, debt, and pensions are protected, which leaves about $693 million the City could legally cut — enough to cover the loss on paper. But that is the budget for libraries, parks, grants, indigent care, and transfers, and much of it is one-time money that won't offset a loss returning every year.

2 · Where the money has gone

Most of the recent growth went to public safety

From 2015 to 2024, about 83% of the real, inflation-adjusted growth in city spending went to public safety — the Sheriff's Office, Fire and Rescue, and the Medical Examiner. Across everything else, inflation-adjusted spending per resident has been roughly flat or slightly down.

3 · The remaining option

The main lever left is the property tax rate

Covering the gap with cuts would mean ending core services, and much of that spending is one-time, so it is not a durable fix. With the local sales surtax and most fees capped by state law, the main lasting revenue lever the City fully controls is the property tax millage rate. Offsetting the full $283 million would mean raising it from about 11.19 mills to roughly 13.85 mills (the consolidated charter allows up to about 20). Put plainly: a measure intended to reduce property taxes would, to be offset locally, require raising the property tax rate.

A working analysis · This is a math question, not a partisan one

A decade of budget growth went to public safety. Amendment 3 cuts what pays for it.

Before you vote on Amendment 3, see what Jacksonville has actually been buying with its budget — and what a $283 million annual cut would put under pressure. From 2015 to 2024, 83% of the city's real spending growth went to the Sheriff's Office and Fire & Rescue, while spending on everything else fell per resident. The proposed homestead exemption would reduce the General Fund by about $283 million a year and replaces none of it. This site lays out the math so you can weigh it for yourself.

1 · Where the money went

Jacksonville's budget growth has gone to expanding public safety

From 2015 to 2024, about 83% of the real, inflation-adjusted growth in city spending went to public safety — the Sheriff's Office, Fire and Rescue, and the Medical Examiner. Across everything else, inflation-adjusted spending per resident fell about 3%. And Jacksonville starts lean: at $2,065 per resident, it spends less than any of the 13 peer cities we compared, roughly $1,700 below the median. The budget has not been growing government in general — it has been buying police and fire protection.

2 · The core problem

Covering the cut means cutting deep — and “permitted use” is not funding

The proposed exemption would reduce Jacksonville's General Fund revenue by about $283 million a year and replaces none of it. The amendment lists public safety as a permitted use of the remaining property tax, but it guarantees no funding level — police, fire, and their pensions are paid from the same shrinking fund. On paper, about $691 million of non-protected spending could be cut to cover the loss. But that is the budget for libraries, parks, grants, indigent care, and transfers — already flat-to-down per resident for a decade — and much of it is one-time money that won't offset a loss returning every year.

3 · What first responders are saying

The people who run public safety are raising the alarm

Florida's statewide law-enforcement, firefighter, and fire-chief organizations — the Florida Sheriffs Association, the Florida State Fraternal Order of Police, the Florida Professional Firefighters, and the Florida Fire Chiefs' Association — have publicly opposed or raised formal concerns about Amendment 3. Their concern is the same math this site walks through: the amendment removes local revenue without replacing it, and no list of “permitted uses” pays a deputy's salary.

Florida Sheriffs Association, August 2026

“The Florida Sheriffs Association is significantly concerned with Constitutional Amendment 3, which potentially allows Tallahassee to control your local budgets, creates longer law enforcement response times, limits communities' abilities to provide road repairs and stormwater removal — these are a few things voters deserve to be aware of.”

Here in Jacksonville

Sheriff T.K. Waters has said he will not reduce JSO's requested $38 million budget increase for next year — a request he describes as maintaining current operations, not expansion: “When we run an organization with 3,500 plus employees, you have to pay them… over 80% of our budget is salary and benefits… We're not over-asking. We didn't ask for anything last year. We're not asking for much this year.” That commitment — and the Police & Fire pension plan, only 47.2% funded — is paid from the same General Fund the amendment would shrink by $283 million a year.

4 · The remaining option

The main lever left is the property tax rate

Covering the gap with cuts would mean ending core services, and much of that spending is one-time, so it is not a durable fix. With the local sales surtax and most fees capped by state law, the main lasting revenue lever the City fully controls is the property tax millage rate. Offsetting the full $283 million would mean raising it from about 11.19 mills to roughly 13.85 mills (the consolidated charter allows up to about 20). Put plainly: a measure intended to reduce property taxes would, to be offset locally, require raising the property tax rate.

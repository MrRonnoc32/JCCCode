Where the money comes from

Property tax is the single largest source. The other pieces, such as state-shared money and utility taxes, are smaller and largely set outside the City's control.

What's left to cut

A $2 billion budget, and what is left to cut

The City's General Fund is about $2 billion. The proposed exemption would reduce General Fund revenue by roughly $283 million each year. Could the City simply trim $283 million of spending? On paper, yes — once you set aside public safety, debt, and pensions, about $693 million of spending remains. But that pool is what funds libraries, parks, grants, indigent care, and transfers, and much of it is one-time money.

The non-protected pool is larger than the $283M loss — so cuts could close it. But it pays for libraries, parks, grants, indigent care, and transfers, and a large share is one-time money, so cutting it does not durably replace a loss that returns every year.

In short: public safety, debt, and pensions are protected, which leaves about $693 million — roughly a third of the budget — that the City could legally choose to cut. That is more than the $283 million loss, but it is the City's discretionary spending: libraries, parks, neighborhood services, grants, and health and community programs. And because much of it is nonrecurring, cutting it closes the gap once, not for the years that follow.
